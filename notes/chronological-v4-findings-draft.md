This report follows `replay-run-74182b3044644ba4ac7f741822056a57`. Four peers examined sampled arriving correspondence from the perspectives of routines, expectations, expertise, and contrasts. Their neutral objective was to identify implicit organizational practice. They were not supplied a fraud target or the retrospective case queries used in earlier experiments.

The account distinguishes **recorded model observations** from **reviewer synthesis**. A source-backed hypothesis remains a hypothesis: exact quotations establish what the archive contains, not why people acted, how widespread a practice was, or whether an inferred procedure would help another agent perform a new task. References below identify the actual documents and immutable body spans. Reviewer inspection of longer source context is labeled separately from what the model selected.

Dates in section headings are **virtual discovery dates**: the end of the arrival window when the swarm first recorded the relevant material. They are not necessarily the dates of the underlying emails. Large batches delay inspection, and older forwarded statements become available only with their outer message. No claim is made that these patterns would have been discovered at the same dates in real time.

## February 18, 2000: remembered compromises behind an awkward product description

In window 1, the expertise peer proposed that paper/pulp product descriptions were negotiated with traders and that Legal relied on memory of earlier compromises. The routines peer repeated the hypothesis during revision. Their source juxtaposed a trader's objection to volume wording with a lawyer's recollection of why imperfect wording had been retained. Both passages came from one exchange, not two independent episodes. [S1].

The useful detail is the **exception rationale**. The source says the parties had not been fully satisfied with the Notional Quantity language, but intended the day-versus-month volume basis to be clear elsewhere in the longer description. A complaint about confusing multiplication therefore could not be evaluated from the disputed phrase alone. Its interpretation depended on another part of the document and on remembering an earlier compromise. The succinct assessment was: “Not elegant, but adequate.” [S1].

Reviewer inspection of the full cited message found an additional explicit request to check previous emails for the latest acceptable version, coupled with uncertainty about the remembered agreement. This strengthens a narrow interpretation: in this episode, correspondence and a participant's memory helped recover both an accepted version and the reason for a workaround. It does not establish that email was the only record, that no formal version control existed, or that this was the firm's normal process. The model-selected evidence alone supports less than a company-wide claim about how Legal worked.

A potentially transferable check follows from that interpretation: when revising a locally awkward clause, identify the other clauses or conventions that compensate for it before simplifying it. That is **reviewer-proposed procedure abstraction**, not a procedure the experiment validated. Its test would involve a different later revision: does inspecting the compensating context avert a demonstrable unit or interpretation error? Merely finding another email containing the trader's name would not answer that question.

The same discovery window raised a second lead about clearing arrangements. The model described reliance on an experienced operational contact, Maria Nartey, whose reply discussed current clearing houses and impressions of their back-office performance. Its claim that this amounted to a standing informal vetting practice was too broad. A single exchange identifies a consulted person and her reported experience, not a repeated selection procedure or an actual final decision. [S2].

The more specific coordination issue is that clearing documents already named a presumed clearer and had been sent for signature while the participants were still reconciling that assumption with London practice. Reviewer reading of the longer thread also found explicit role descriptions, so the contact's expertise was not wholly hidden knowledge discovered from behavior. The supported question is whether legal documentation, regional operating arrangements, and exchange assumptions had reached the same state of readiness. The correspondence does not reveal the eventual choice. [S2].

## May 5, 2000: different participants meant different things by “ready”

Window 2 surfaced a PG&E documentation exchange. A commercial-side inquiry conveyed that the client believed everything had been provided and that the next move belonged to Enron. The reply identified both work still owed by Enron and documents still expected from the counterparty, including a blackline controlled by the counterparty. [S3].

The model's observation overstated this as a practice of waiting and not initiating changes whenever the counterparty controlled drafting. The quoted source itself says Enron still needed to respond to comments. Thus the evidence does not support passive deference. It supports **simultaneous dependencies**: one party can owe a response while still lacking another input needed to finish.

Together with the clearing correspondence, this suggests a higher-level question: when someone says a transaction is ready, which components are they certifying? Commercial agreement, document version, external supporting material, and operational setup may advance separately. This cross-source connection is reviewer synthesis. The model did not demonstrate a general readiness algorithm or validate the connection on a held-out transaction.

A useful next observation would be a dated handoff explicitly listing completed and outstanding components, followed by what the recipient actually did. That would distinguish a misunderstood handoff from an avoidable delay or a genuinely missing input. The current sources justify asking for that evidence, not choosing among those explanations.

## June 30–August 16, 2000: requests, explicit local policy, and referral knowledge

Some later cards illustrated why the label “observation” is necessary but insufficient. Window 3 included a request to give a new attorney database access and report back when it was granted. The model turned that into access being granted without formal approval. Neither the outcome nor the absence of an approval process is shown by the request. The defensible observation is simply that a colleague routed an access request to a named recipient. [S4].

The personal-days correspondence provides a different distinction. It reports checking HR and the policy manual, recognizing supervisory discretion, and announcing a specific Research Group arrangement. This is **explicit local policy clarification**, not an unwritten rule recovered from indirect evidence. Reviewer reading of the complete message confirms that it also specifies notice and recording arrangements. Its value for a knowledge system is keeping the general policy, authorized local discretion, and local implementation separate; a flat summary of either “no personal days” or “two personal days” would lose that scope. [S5].

In window 4, an interviewer scheduling problem elicited two suggested substitutes after a requested person was unavailable. This is a small, concrete example of **knowing whom to ask**: the respondent supplied alternatives not contained in the original scheduling request. But it does not establish interchangeability, qualifications, appointment, or a standing staffing rule for trading groups. A later successful substitution or a stated reason for rejecting a suggested person would be the discriminating evidence. [S6].
