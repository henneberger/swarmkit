                                                                                           Abstract
                                                    As language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents, they must
                                                    coordinate with others over long horizons in open-ended interactive tasks. Yet
                                                    existing evaluations rarely test these demands together, instead emphasising single-
                                                    agent tasks, short interactions, or highly structured multi-agent settings. We
                                                    introduce ALEM, a JAX-based benchmark for open-ended multi-agent coordi-
                                                    nation built on Craftax-like dynamics. ALEM embeds procedurally generated
                                                    coordination tasks, soft specialisation, communication, and controllable coordi-
                                                    nation difficulty into a long-horizon survival world with exploration, crafting,
                                                    trading, and combat. We evaluate 13 modern LLMs zero-shot within homogeneous
                                                    teams, with trained MARL agents as reference points. Current LLM agents re-
                                                    main far from solving ALEM, averaging only ∼ 6% normalised return, but their
                                                    failures are not uniform. On the hardest coordination setting, zero-shot Gemini-
                                                    3.1-Pro-High approaches MARL agents trained for one billion steps, while GPT-
                                                    5.4-High achieves strong base-task reward but much lower coordination reward.
                                                    This contrast shows that individual task competence does not imply coordination
                                                    competence. Ablations show that communication is the largest contributor to
                                                    coordination, while memory and reasoning help when used to maintain multi-
                                                    step plans. Overall, our results identify coordination as a distinct bottleneck for
                                                    frontier LLM agents, separate from single-agent capabilities. ALEM makes this
                                                    bottleneck measurable and provides a controlled testbed for developing agents
                                                    that communicate, allocate roles, and execute shared plans. Code is available at
                                                    https://github.com/alem-world/alem-env.


                                        1     Introduction
                                        Large Language Models (LLMs) have evolved rapidly, from systems primarily used for text
                                        generation [8, 57, 13] to agentic systems capable of completing long-horizon, open-ended
                                        tasks [31, 62, 11, 35, 39, 59]. As these systems are deployed as autonomous agents, they will
                                        increasingly operate in multi-agent settings where success depends on coordinating with others [25].
                                        Yet the mechanisms required for sustained multi-agent coordination remain poorly understood, es-
                                        pecially when agents must communicate intent, allocate roles, maintain shared plans, and align
                                        actions over long horizons [23]. This creates a need to evaluate whether LLM agents can coordinate
                                        effectively in long-horizon, open-ended multi-agent environments, and to characterise the failures
                                        that arise when they cannot.
                                        Existing benchmarks cover only part of this space. MultiAgentBench, Collab-Overcooked, and
                                        related multi-agent LLM benchmarks evaluate collaboration, but typically in shorter-horizon and
                                        more narrowly specified tasks [69, 53, 3, 60, 22, 38]. Others focus on long-horizon task completion in
                                        primarily single-agent settings [42, 67, 49, 61, 68, 27, 20], or include multiple agents but lack explicit

To address these gaps, we introduce ALEM2 (Fig. 1), a benchmark for open-ended3 multi-agent coordi-
nation built on Craftax-like dynamics [37, 40]. ALEM procedurally generates complex environments
where coordination difficulty is modulated by a single parameter, ranging from loose temporal depen-
dencies and handovers to tightly coupled joint actions (Fig. 2). Information-theoretic diagnostics show
that these mechanics induce genuine inter-agent dependence, not just added single-agent difficulty
(App. B.1). Implemented in JAX [6], the benchmark facilitates scalable and reproducible evaluation
of how multi-agent failure modes emerge as coordination demands increase.
Using ALEM, we evaluate 13 modern LLMs in zero-shot homogeneous teams, with trained MARL
agents as reference points. Current LLM agents remain far from solving the benchmark, averaging
only ∼ 6% normalised return. Gemini-3.1-Pro-High performs comparably to MARL agents trained
for one billion environment steps on the hardest coordination setting (17.5% vs. 17.6% Coord.%),
while GPT-5.4-High achieves strong base-task reward but much lower coordination reward. This
contrast shows that individual task competence is not sufficient for coordinated progress. Ablations
further show that communication has the largest effect on coordination performance, while memory
and reasoning help when they support multi-step planning. Finally, in initial heterogeneous-team
experiments, mixed teams perform near the average of their constituent homogeneous baselines rather
than matching the strongest member’s performance. Together, these results identify long-horizon,
open-ended coordination as a distinct bottleneck for current LLM agents.

coordination with procedural tasks, soft specialisation, communication, and controllable difficulty; ii)
pixel, symbolic, and text interfaces for evaluating MARL and zero-shot LLM agents under matched
coordination demands; and iii) a systematic evaluation of 13 modern LLMs and 4 MARL baselines,

2      Background
We model the environment as a partially observable stochastic game (POSG) [26], denoted as
the tuple ⟨I, S, A, O, Ω, T, R⟩. Here, I = {1, . . . , n} is the set of agents, S is the set of states,
A = A1 × · · · × An is the joint action space, and O = O1 × · · · × On is the joint observation space.
The observation function Ω = (Ω1 , . . . , Ωn ) maps states to per-agent observations: at each state
s ∈ S, agent i receives an individual observation oi = Ωi (s) ∈ Oi and selects an action ai ∈ Ai .
The environment transitions according to T : S × A → ∆(S), where ∆(·) is the set of probability
distributions over a set. Each agent receives an individual reward given by Ri : S × A × S → R.

3      ALEM : An Open-Ended World for Multi-Agent Coordination

We introduce ALEM, a benchmark for studying coordination in open-ended multi-agent worlds.
Built on Craftax-Coop [40], ALEM extends prior work along four axes: diverse coordination types,
procedural generation of coordination tasks, soft specialisation, and an explicit communication
channel. Across nine procedurally generated levels, agents explore, trade resources, craft tools, build
structures, and fight mobs under controllable coordination demands. By scaling these demands while
preserving the underlying world structure, ALEM enables controlled evaluation of how coordination
failures emerge as coordination demands increase. Information-theoretic diagnostics verify that these
mechanics require genuine inter-agent dependence rather than added single-agent difficulty [56]
(App. B.1), while our JAX implementation supports billion-step MARL training on a single GPU in
under two days (App. C.3).

3.1      Coordination Types

Following Malone and Crowston [36], we view coordination as interdependence between activities. In
multi-agent systems, these activities correspond to agents’ actions. We characterise the dependencies
between coupled actions Ait and Ajt′ by their temporal separation, ∆t = |t − t′ |, resulting in a
coordination spectrum from long-range dependencies to simultaneous joint action (Fig. 2).
Existing MARL benchmarks typically isolate specific regions of this spectrum: SMAC [45] empha-
sises synchronous focus-fire (∆t ≈ 0), Overcooked [10] tests short-window handovers (small ∆t),
and Melting Pot’s Clean Up [1] targets long-range resource interdependence (large ∆t). ALEM instan-
tiates this entire spectrum within a single open-ended environment, using procedural generation to
vary both the temporal structure of coordination and the number of agents involved (details App. B.2).

3.2     Procedural Coordination Generation

Existing environments procedurally generate spatial layouts to reduce memorisation and encourage
generalisation [40, 37, 65, 24]. However, if coordination requirements are fixed, agents can still
exploit recurring interaction patterns. ALEM therefore procedurally generates the coordination
structure itself.
Each episode, eligible entities across tasks such as mining, construction, combat, and crafting (Fig. 1)
are independently assigned a coordination type (synchronous or handover) and a required agent count
(2 to N ). Synchronous tasks also adopt game-theoretic incentives: hard tasks act as pure coordination
problems requiring joint action for any reward [46], while soft tasks function as assurance games,
balancing a safe solo payoff against a larger joint-action bonus [50] (details App. B.2).

3.3     Soft Specialisation and Difficulty Scaling

Instead of enforcing rigid division of labour via hard role gates [1, 40], ALEM uses soft specialisation.
Any agent can attempt any role-gated action, but specialists succeed deterministically (ηspec = 1)
while non-specialists succeed with probability ηnon = 1 − α. This rewards effective role allocation
while preserving the capacity for online adaptation.
We scale overall coordination difficulty with the single scalar α ∈ [0, 1]. Because world generation in
JAX is deterministic, varying α preserves the spatial layout and frequency of coordination opportuni-
ties (Fig. 3), cleanly isolating execution difficulty from world variation. Higher α tightens execution
constraints: it increases the probability of N -agent requirements, shortens handover windows, in-
creases solo failure rates on soft tasks, and lowers non-specialist efficiency. We evaluate on Easy
(α = 0.3), Medium (α = 0.6), and Hard (α = 0.9) settings (parameters detailed in App. B.3).

3.4     Environment Interface

3.4.1    RL Interface
Observation space. ALEM exposes both pixel (see Fig. 6, App B) and symbolic observations of
the underlying state. Each egocentric observation includes a local map, inventory, and a teammate
dashboard (health, specialisation, requested resources, off-screen direction, and recent communica-
tions). Beyond Craftax-Coop, ALEM explicitly embeds communication messages, coordination states,
including soft/hard requirements, handover countdowns, and multi-agent mob targets.

Action space. Agents act in a 60-dimensional discrete action space encompassing environment
manipulation, crafting, exploration, and combat. To facilitate teamwork, agents can issue persistent
resource requests (active for up to 10 timesteps), execute teammate-targeted giving, and broadcast
discrete messages. The primary D O action is contextual, it supports both environment interaction and


                                                     4
direct agent-to-agent mechanics, such as reviving downed teammates or triggering friendly fire. We
apply conservative action masking to filter strictly infeasible choices.

Achievements and rewards. Following prior environments [37, 40], ALEM pairs a dense health
reward with sparse, first-time rewards for completing a hierarchy of achievements. We introduce 27
novel coordination achievements spanning synchronised mining, temporal handovers, cooperative
construction, and coordinated combat. Rewards are individual by default; coordination bonuses are
awarded only to participating agents by default (details in App. C.6).

3.4.2    Text-based Language Interface
To evaluate language agents, we expose a structured text interface mapped directly to the underlying
environment (details in App. C.5). At each timestep, models receive a textual state containing
recent observation-action history, teammate messages, a private scratchpad, and visible coordination
constraints, together with explicit action affordances. Models must return a structured response
containing exactly one valid action and, optionally, reasoning, communication, and scratchpad fields.
This parsed action is then mapped to the corresponding discrete environment step. A sample prompt
is provided in App. C.7.

3.5     MARL Baselines

We train IPPO [12], MAPPO [66], PQN-VDN [16], and HyperMARL-IPPO [55] to provide reference
points for zero-shot LLM performance and to verify that the difficulty settings meaningfully affect
learned policies. Figure 3 reports 1B-step performance (hyperparameter sweeps detailed in App. C.1).
Performance sharply degrades as coordination difficulty increases; even after 3B environment steps,
the strongest baseline reaches only 18% of the maximum total reward on the Hard setting (App. C.4).
These results show that ALEM remains an unsaturated challenge for current MARL methods.

4     Experiments
We evaluate 13 modern LLMs in zero-shot homogeneous teams, with 4 trained MARL baselines as
reference points. Our goal is to measure coordination in long-horizon, open-ended environments,
while separating coordination difficulty from the difficulty of the underlying world. To calibrate this
distinction, we compare ALEM against a single-agent baseline and find that its multi-agent dynamics
result in a drop in performance, showing that coordination introduces a distinct layer of difficulty
(App. E.1).
Our experiments ask three questions: Q1: How well do LLM agents coordinate zero-shot within
homogeneous teams? Q2: How do communication, memory, and reasoning affect coordination? Q3:
How well do agents coordinate in heterogeneous teams composed of different models?

4.1     Experimental Setup

Evaluation protocol. We follow a zero-shot evaluation protocol similar to BALROG [42]. At each
timestep, each agent receives the game rules, environment details, local observation, and legal actions
in text form through the interface described in Sec. 3.4.2. By default, agents have scratchpad memory,
broadcast communication, and a text history containing the last eight observations and actions.
Success in ALEM requires spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning, tracking crafting dependencies,
memory, effective communication, and coordination under partial observability.

Metrics. Our primary metric is normalised episode return, reported as a percentage of the maximum
achievable reward. Following previous Craftax-based environments [40, 37], each agent’s cumulative
reward is normalised by the maximum reward for the relevant category and averaged across agents.
We report Base%, Coord.%, and Total%, each normalised independently, so Total% is not the sum of
Base% and Coord.%. Reward bounds are given in App. C.2.

Models. We evaluate 13 modern language models. Closed-source models are evaluated via
their respective APIs (GPT-5.4 [41] and Gemini 3.1 Pro [18]). Open-weight models are served
with vLLM [32] and include Gemma-4-E4B-it/26B-A4B-it/31B-it [19], Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct

4.2      Results

4.2.1       Q1: How well do LLM agents coordinate zero-shot within homogeneous teams?
ALEM is unsolved but separates models. Table 1 shows that ALEM remains far from saturated
by both zero-shot LLMs and trained MARL baselines, while separating models across a broad
performance range. On Easy, Total% spans from 0.8% for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to 18.1% for
Gemini-3.1-Pro-High; on Hard, LLMs average only 6.2% Total%. Yet the top model, Gemini-3.1-
Pro-High, reaches 17.5% Coord.% on Hard, comparable to the 17.6% Coord.% of MARL agents
trained for one billion environment steps. Many models also survive for long episodes despite low
returns (Fig. 4c), suggesting that the main bottleneck is not basic survival or interface parsing (parsing
rates in Fig. 12, App. C.2), but converting interaction time into coordinated progress.

Base competence does not guarantee coordination. Decomposing performance reveals failures
hidden by aggregate scores. GPT-5.4-High illustrates this separation: despite achieving the second-
highest Base% across difficulties (14.1%, 10.0%, 11.8%), its Coord.% (7.0%, 4.0%, 4.2%) remains
much lower, lagging behind smaller models such as Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it on Hard with non-
overlapping 95% CIs. Fig. 4b shows that GPT-5.4-High has the highest trade count (48.9) but
relatively few revives (3.4), whereas Gemini-3.1-Pro-High is more balanced (29.3 trades, 6.9 revives).
Thus, local cooperative actions and base-task progress do not necessarily translate into sustained
coordination. Conversely, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it achieve higher Coord.%
than Base%, indicating that coordination can be exploited even when general competence is limited.

Coordination failures differ across task structure. The task-level breakdown in Fig. 4a shows
that coordination is not a single axis. Handover tasks are comparatively more accessible because
they allow temporal slack, as one agent can initiate an opportunity and another can complete it within
a bounded time window. By contrast, Synchronous-Hard tasks require agents to identify a shared
target, navigate to compatible positions, communicate intent, and act in the same timestep. This
strict synchronisation compounds the spatial reasoning challenges already observed in single-agent
LLMs [42]. The results reflect this difficulty, as Gemini-3.1-Pro-High leads LLMs on synchronous-
hard coordination with only 18% coverage, while the next-best tested LLMs, Gemma-4-31B-it and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, reach 9%.

Pretrained priors offer advantages in sparse, multi-stage coordination. Construction tasks
require long-horizon planning in addition to coordinated execution. Agents must collect resources,
progress through a technology chain, converge on a shared site, and execute a synchronised build. As
shown in Fig. 4a, construction coverage is at most 2% for every LLM except Gemini-3.1-Pro-High
(7%), and 0% for the MARL 1B reference. This suggests that sparse, long-horizon coordination is
difficult to discover through trial-and-error at this MARL compute budget, while pretrained priors
may give stronger LLMs a limited advantage.

Scale alone does not explain coordination performance. Across open-weight models, perfor-
mance is not monotonic in parameter count. Even within model families such as Gemma-4 and
Qwen-3.5/3.6, coordination performance does not scale linearly across dense and mixture-of-experts
variants (Figs. 4a, 4c). This suggests that coordination depends on factors beyond raw scale, including
possibly post-training, reasoning, and how effectively agents use communication.

Base% are smaller and less consistent (Gemini 13.8 → 11.6, Gemma 7.3 → 8.3), suggesting that
communication primarily supports coordination rather than general environment progress. Message
statistics support this interpretation: agents frequently address specific teammates (Gemini 43.4%,
Gemma 44.1% of messages) and often use imperative verbs (Gemini 73.7%, Gemma 52.2%). Agents
therefore appear to use communication to broadcast intent, assign actions, and align timing. Without
communication, they can still make base progress, but struggle to coordinate effectively (qualitative
analysis in App. I).


Scratchpad memory helps when used for planning. The memory ablation is model-dependent.
Removing scratchpad memory lowers Gemini-3.1-Pro-High’s mean Coord.% from 17.5 to 13.1,
while leaving Base% nearly unchanged (13.8 → 13.6). For Gemma-4-31B-it, the mean changes
are small in Coord.% (8.8 → 8.6) and Base% (7.3 → 8.6), with overlapping confidence intervals.
This suggests that scratchpad memory is not uniformly useful; its effect depends on how models
use it. Qualitative analysis provides a possible explanation (App. G). Gemini uses the scratchpad
as a forward-looking planner: 71.5% of entries are multi-line, 75.3% contain future-oriented terms
such as will, next, then, or plan, and 12.4% contain explicit turn-indexed action sequences such as
T9:Do, T10:Move West, and T11:Give wood to Agent 1. In contrast, Gemma’s scratchpads are
mostly one-line state summaries (average 1.08 lines), with frequent coordinate references (98.8%)
and status information that is often already present in the next observation. These patterns suggest
that scratchpad memory appears more helpful when models use it to preserve plans or commitments
across turns, rather than to restate the current state.


Reasoning supports both coordination and base progress. Reasoning is the only ablation that
lowers both Base% and Coord.% for both models (Fig. 5). For Gemini-3.1-Pro-High, the ablation is
partial because the API only allows us to reduce reasoning, not disable it. Even so, Coord.% falls
from 17.5 to 9.7 and Base% from 13.8 to 10.3. For Gemma-4-31B-it, disabling reasoning reduces
Coord.% from 8.8 to 5.5 and Base% from 7.3 to 4.6.
Qualitative traces suggest different failure modes (App. H). For Gemini, reduced reasoning removes
much of the planner-like behaviour: scratchpad length falls from 207 to 69 characters, turn-indexed
plans nearly disappear (12.4% → 0.1%), and messages become shorter (132 → 53 characters).
Gemma instead writes longer scratchpads without reasoning (153 → 228 characters), often near the
∼ 1000-character cap, suggesting that the scratchpad partly substitutes for the removed reasoning.
This compensation is incomplete, as both Base% and Coord.% still decrease.

4.2.3   Q3: How well do agents coordinate in teams composed of different models?
We evaluate heterogeneous teams on Hard in two settings: a same-family Gemma team composed of
Gemma-4-E4B-it, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, and Gemma-4-31B-it, and a cross-family team composed
of the three strongest LLM agents: Gemma-4-31B-it, GPT-5.4-High, and Gemini-3.1-Pro-High.

Heterogeneous teams perform near the homogeneous-team average. Fig. 5 shows that het-
erogeneous teams perform close to the average of their corresponding homogeneous teams. The
same-family Gemma team is slightly above this average (+0.1 Total%), while the cross-family team
is slightly below it (−0.1 Total%). Thus, in the tested settings, heterogeneous teams neither collapse
to the weakest member nor inherit the performance of the strongest one. This suggests that adding a
stronger model to a team is not sufficient to lift team performance. Mixed teams may face additional
coordination problems, as agents must align their communication conventions, planning horizons,
and actions with teammates that reason and communicate differently.

Summary. Overall, ALEM reveals coordination as a bottleneck distinct from base-task performance.
Communication matters most among the ablated harness components, and initial heterogeneous-
team experiments suggest that mixed teams do not automatically match their strongest member’s
performance.

5   Related Work
Long-horizon and agentic evaluation. LLMs are increasingly evaluated as agents in long-horizon,
open-ended tasks [14, 31, 62, 11]. Recent benchmarks test models in complex environments requiring
spatial reasoning, exploration, planning, and tool use [42, 67, 68, 27]. Procedural generation is
especially useful because it reduces memorisation, mitigates contamination, and supports scalable
variation across tasks [42, 48]. ALEM brings these principles to multi-agent evaluation, where agents
must also infer and satisfy diverse coordination demands.

Multi-Agent LLM collaboration. Multi-agent LLMs have shown promise in code generation,
debate, and collaborative reasoning [28, 14]. Recent benchmarks evaluate LLM agents in collaborative
settings [69, 53, 22], but typically use shorter-horizon tasks where roles, goals, or interaction structure
are specified in advance. In contrast, ALEM tests whether LLM agents can infer and execute changing
coordination requirements in a long-horizon open-ended world.

MARL and coordination. MARL benchmarks often focus on particular forms of coordination:
SMAC [45] emphasises synchronous focus-fire, Overcooked [10] tests short-window handovers, and
Melting Pot [1] includes long-range resource interdependence. ALEM builds on Craftax-Coop [40],
but turns coordination into an explicit, procedural, and controllable part of the environment: tasks
are sampled across a coordination spectrum (Fig. 2), agents use soft rather than hard specialisation,
communication is explicit, and difficulty can be scaled. We further use information-theoretic diagnos-
tics [56] to verify that our environment demands genuine multi-agent interdependence (App. B.1).

6   Conclusions
We introduce ALEM, a JAX-based benchmark for long-horizon, open-ended multi-agent coordination.
Our zero-shot evaluations of 13 LLMs alongside trained MARL baselines show that coordination is a
distinct bottleneck for current agents, base-task competence does not guarantee coordinated progress,
and communication has the largest effect on coordinated behaviour. By exposing failure modes across
communication, memory, reasoning, and team composition, ALEM makes coordination measurable
and provides a controlled testbed for developing agents that coordinate effectively.

Limitations and Future Work. Our evaluation focuses on text-based LLM agents, leaving Vision-
Language Models (VLMs) and Claude-family models for future work. API costs limit evaluations of
proprietary models (GPT and Gemini) to 10 seeds per difficulty. Future work can use ALEM to study
stronger agent harnesses, cross-episode memory, and lifelong learning in interactive multi-agent
worlds.

A    ALEM ’s Place Among Existing Environments

Table 2 compares ALEM with representative interactive multi-agent environments. We compare
environments using four criteria. Long horizon means that success requires maintaining progress
over extended trajectories, with rewards delayed by T timesteps where T is typically in the hundreds
or thousands. Bounded open-endedness means that the environment supports a diverse but finite
set of achievable goals G = {g1 , . . . , gn }. This differs from fully open-ended systems, where n
can grow over time through the generation of novel and learnable goals [29]. Explicit coordination
requirements means that the environment contains identifiable multi-agent task structures, such
as synchronous actions, handovers, role allocation, or long-range interdependence. Coordination
difficulty control means that the benchmark exposes an explicit parameter or protocol for scaling
coordination difficulty.
Existing environments cover important parts of this space, but not the full combination targeted
by ALEM. Classic MARL benchmarks such as SMAC, Overcooked, and Hanabi isolate useful
coordination motifs, but are short-horizon and task-specific. Open-world environments such as
Neural MMO and Craftax-Coop provide longer horizons and bounded open-endedness, but do not
expose explicit controls for coordination difficulty. ALEM combines these ingredients by adding
procedurally generated coordination tasks, soft specialisation, communication, and a difficulty
parameter that scales coordination demands while preserving the underlying world structure.
The closest environments are Multi-Agent Craftax v1 [65] and Craftax-Coop [40]. Both inherit
Craftax’s long-horizon, bounded open-ended structure, but their coordination demands are mostly
implicit. Multi-Agent Craftax v1 places multiple agents in the same open world, but does not
introduce explicit coordination tasks, a controllable coordination-difficulty axis, or the full nine-level
progression used in Craftax and ALEM (it is implemented only on the overworld). Craftax-Coop adds
roles, creating long-range interdependence through specialisation. However, it does not procedurally
generate coordination requirements over entities, require synchronous or handover coordination,
provide an explicit communication channel, or expose a parameter that scales coordination difficulty
while preserving the same world. ALEM adds these mechanisms directly: coordination tasks are
sampled each episode, span synchronous and handover structures, vary the required number of agents,
and are controlled by a single difficulty parameter.

B.1     Probing Environment Changes

We use the information-theoretic diagnostics proposed in [56] to check whether the design changes in
ALEM introduce the intended behavioural demands. We roll out trained RL policies under the same
four settings used in the environment calibration: a single-agent setting, a multi-agent setting without
role pressure or coordination tasks, a multi-agent setting with soft specialisation, and full ALEM
(E ASY). The diagnostics are reported as behavioural questions in Table 3, with the corresponding
metric acronym shown in parentheses.
All four diagnostic signals increase as multi-agent structure is added. This suggests that full ALEM
induces stronger demands for memory use, hidden teammate information, synchronous coordination,
and temporal coordination, rather than simply making the base game harder. We report diagnostic
magnitudes rather than Wilcoxon tests because this is a single-environment calibration across settings,
not a suite-level comparison across independent scenarios.

C.5                               Text-based Language Interface Details

Prompt Structure. At each timestep t, the prompt for agent i is assembled from three components:
a system prompt, a history of recent observations and actions, and the current observation with
instructions.

                              1. System prompt: The system prompt is shared across all time steps for an agent and
                                 defines the agent identity and role, the team size, the objective, and the rules needed to act
                                 in the environment. By default it also optionally includes the full action catalogue with
                                 natural-language descriptions, game and coordination mechanics.
                              2. Observation and Action History: The observation and action history is a rolling sequence
                                 of recent messages from the environment and the agent itself. The observation messages
                                 contain previous observations, labelled as “Observation from k step(s) ago”, and the action
                                 messages contain the action previously taken by the agent. When memory and commu-
                                 nication is enabled, the observation history also includes scratchpad notes and teammate
                                 messages, each labelled with the corresponding timestep.
                              3. Current Observation and Action Space: The current observation message contains textual
                                 descriptions of: achievement progress, current level, nearby terrain, items and enemies,
                                 visible coordination opportunities and requirements, teammate status, agent stats, vitals
                                 and inventory. The agent is then prompted to respond with an action. By default, the
                                 call to action prompt also includes current legal action affordances, instructions to think
                                 step-by-step before acting, to optionally broadcast a message, and to optionally write a
                                 private scratchpad note.

The length of observation, communication, and memory history is independently configurable, and
the system prompt can be ablated to control how much information about the game and coordination
mechanics is disclosed to the model. The default setting includes all information in the system prompt
and current observation with communication and scratchpad history enabled, but we ablate these
components to probe their effects on LLM performance in 4.2.2.

Prompt Disclosure Levels. In order to reduce the amount of information given to the model, we
only reveal specific gameplay information as the team reaches the relevant level.


                                                                                                                 26
Coordination Information. The current observation includes explicit natural language descriptions
of the current coordination targets and requirements. For synchronous targets, the prompt states
whether the target works solo with a bonus or requires N agents simultaneously, what the required
action is, and which side of the target the acting agent currently occupies. The location and vitals of
teammates are included as well as their previous messages.

Action Parsing and Execution. The model response is instructed to respond in a structured format
containing exactly one action tag, optionally preceded by a think tag for reasoning, communication
tags for teammate messages, and scratchpad tags for private notes. The required action is parsed from
the response and must match one of the legal actions. If parsing fails, the agent is re-prompted with
a format error. If the model still fails to produce a valid action after the configured retry budget is
exhausted, the action defaults to N OOP. The final parsed action is mapped back to the underlying
discrete action index before stepping the environment.

 system

 You are Agent 1 (forager) in a 3-agent cooperative survival game. Your goal is to gather resources,
      craft gear, fight monsters, and descend through 9 dungeon levels, while coordinating with
      teammates. You must survive -- if your health reaches zero, you die, and if all agents die the
      game ends. Maximize the number of achievements while staying alive.

 <game_rules>

 ## How to play
 - Each turn, choose exactly one action.
 - **Movement** uses absolute directions: north, south, east, and west. Any move attempt changes your
      facing to that direction, even if the move is blocked and you stay in place. A move is blocked
      if the target tile is solid, including trees, stone, ore veins, walls, crafting stations, chests
      , and plants, or if it contains water, lava, a mob, or another player. If repeated move attempts
       do not change your position, that direction is blocked. You can also use a blocked move to turn
       in place, for example to face an adjacent tree.
 - **Facing**: your facing direction is set by your last movement action and persists until you move
      again. **Do** always targets the tile in your current facing direction.
 - **Do** is your main interaction: face a tile and use the **Do** action on exactly that tile to chop
       trees, mine ore, attack creatures, drink water, open chests, or revive a downed teammate. If
      the faced tile contains a downed teammate, Do revives them. If the faced tile contains a living
      teammate, Do targets that teammate instead and can cause friendly fire. For synchronous-style
      coordination, all required agents must stand next to the same target tile, face it, and act
      together.
 - **Crafting**: stand next to (including diagonally) the required station and use the craft action;
      you do NOT need to face it. Diamond items always require an adjacent epic forge, not a table.
 - **Placing**: face the target tile, then use the place action. Tables and furnaces need an empty non-
      solid tile that is not water or lava; stone can also be placed into water (costs 1 stone). Place
       Plant puts a sapling on the faced tile. Place Torch lights dark areas.
 - **Ranged combat**: use Shoot Arrow while facing a creature (requires a bow + arrows). Bows are
      found in dungeon chests.
 - **Elite mobs** are tougher and deal more damage; coordinating with teammates (multiple agents
      attacking together) makes them much easier to defeat.
 - **Request/Give**: use Request [Resource] to broadcast a resource request to teammates for 10 turns;
       teammates can use Give to Agent X to transfer one unit of the requested resource directly -- no
       adjacency required, works at any distance. Give only appears as an available action when a
      teammate has an active Request.

 ## Survival stats
 Food, drink, and energy deplete gradually over time -- roughly every 20-30 steps you lose 1 point of
      each (dexterity slows this rate). When food or drink reaches 0, your health starts dropping.
      When energy reaches 0, you automatically fall asleep and cannot act until energy is full. While
      sleeping, you take 2.5x damage from all sources. Mana does NOT decay -- it is only spent by
      casting spells or enchanting. Mana slowly regenerates over time (faster while sleeping).
 - **Sleep**: choose this voluntarily to recover energy at 2x the passive rate. Ends automatically
      when energy is full.
 - **Rest**: choose this to recover health gradually. Requires food, drink, and energy all > 0; ends
      when health is full or a stat runs out.

 ## Roles
 Role-restricted actions succeed with reduced probability for non-specialists. Depending on the
      difficulty configuration, non-specialist success rates are 10%

 - **Forager**: collecting water, saplings, eating passive mobs (e.g. cows/bats/snails). Also has 3x
      base food and drink capacity.
 - **Miner**: crafting pickaxes/torches, placing stone.
 - **Warrior**: crafting swords and arrows. Also deals 2x melee damage, and specializes in enchanting
      swords and bows.
 - No role restriction: Place Table, Place Furnace, Wood Sword, Iron Armour, Diamond Armour

 ## Coordination
 Some tasks, tiles, creatures, and structures require multiple agents. When collaboration is possible
      the observation will include a short coordination hint. Follow the rules below to coordinate
      safely and efficiently.

 - **Sync**: N agents must each stand on a different tile adjacent to the shared target, each facing
      it, and all choose the **Do** action on the same turn. Approach from different sides so every
      agent targets the shared tile directly. If a teammate is between you and the target, your **Do**
       will hit the teammate instead and can cause friendly fire.
 - **Handover**: one agent starts the task with **Do** (pays the resources required), and another
      agent finishes it with **Do** within a small time window shown in the observation. If no agent
      completes it in time, the site resets and materials are refunded to the initiator. Follow the
      exact handover timing when specified.




                                                  28
- **Construction**: Construction sites (shelters, forges, beacons) may require either sync or
     handover. Always follow the coordination rule shown in the observation for that site.
- **Elite mobs**: stronger enemies may benefit from or require coordinated attacks. Attack from
     different sides, avoid standing between a teammate and the mob, and avoid blocking another agent
     's attack.
- **Revive**: Face a downed teammate and use **Do** to revive them.
- **Epic forge / Diamond crafting**: Diamond-tier items require multiple agents to craft
     simultaneously at an adjacent epic forge. All required agents must choose the crafting action
     for the same item on the same turn while adjacent to the forge.

E     Additional LLM Experiments and Ablations
E.1   Environment calibration: multi-agent structure adds difficulty beyond the base game.

Before evaluating full ALEM, we isolate how much difficulty is introduced by the multi-agent structure
beyond the underlying open-ended environment dynamics. We evaluate Gemma-4-31B-it in four
settings that share the same base world: a single-agent setting, a multi-agent setting without role
pressure or coordination tasks, a multi-agent setting with soft specialisation, and full ALEM at α = 0.9
(H ARD).
Fig. 19 shows a steady decline in Base reward, the only reward category directly comparable
across all settings: 12.8% in the single-agent setting, 11.4% with multiple agents, 10.1% with soft
specialisation, and 7.3% in full ALEM. The key comparison is the single-agent setting versus full
ALEM : a 43% relative drop on the same underlying world. This calibration suggests that ALEM ’s
difficulty is not reducible to the base game. Adding agents changes the dynamics as each agent’s
transition depends on the actions of the others, soft specialisation adds role-allocation pressure, and
full ALEM adds explicit coordination requirements. We do not over-interpret the intermediate steps,
since adjacent confidence intervals overlap and this calibration uses one open-weight model. Instead,
the result serves as a sanity check that full ALEM introduces multi-agent failure modes absent from
single-agent progression.

G.2   Quantitative results

Table 9 shows that the two models use the memory field in different ways. Gemini tends to write
longer, multi-line entries with explicit future plans. Gemma writes shorter entries that more often
summarise the current state.
Three patterns stand out.

Gemini writes plans; Gemma writes state summaries. The structural differences are large.
Gemini writes 3.86 lines per memory entry on average, compared with 1.08 for Gemma. Similarly,
71.5% of Gemini entries have at least two lines, compared with 2.0% for Gemma. Gemini also


                                                   36
uses explicit turn-indexed plans in 12.4% of entries, while Gemma almost never uses this format
(0.2%). Reading the entries gives the same picture: Gemini often lays out the next few turns line by
line, whereas Gemma usually writes a compact one-line summary of position, recent obstacles, next
movement, and teammate state.

Neither model uses the pad as memory. Both models use the memory entry more for immediate
planning or state tracking than for recording past failures or lessons. Past-tense or retrospective
tokens, such as tried, failed, earlier, before, already, and noticed, appear in only 9.0% of Gemini
entries and 6.7% of Gemma entries. This is notable because the memory entry is one of the few
persistent within-episode channels available to the model. It could be used to record facts such as a
failed craft attempt, a previous handover, or an already-tested route, but the models rarely use it in
this way. This could also be due to the history (last 8 observations and actions) agents receive in each
observation.

Both models actively rewrite their memory entries. The memory entries are not simply copied
forward unchanged. Consecutive controlled entries have token Jaccard similarity of 0.37 for Gemini
and 0.47 for Gemma, with character-level similarity of 0.44 and 0.58, respectively. This indicates
substantial rewriting from one controlled step to the next. Gemini rewrites more than Gemma,
consistent with its planning-oriented usage: it often recomputes the next few actions rather than
incrementally updating a fixed state summary.

H     Reasoning ablation: detailed analysis

H.1   Setup

The reasoning ablation differs across models because of API constraints. Gemini 3.1 Pro does not
allow reasoning to be disabled, so we compare the high-reasoning baseline to a low-reasoning setting,
denoted Gemini-Low. Gemma allows the reasoning trace to be disabled entirely, denoted Gemma-No-
Reasoning. Gemini-Low still records 879 ± 44 characters and 198 ± 20 tokens of raw_reasoning
per controlled step on average, so it should be interpreted as a partial ablation.
We collected 10 Gemini-Low episodes and 18 Gemma-No-Reasoning episodes. We reuse the same
metrics as in App. G for memory entries on controlled steps, and App. I for messages across all
records. The lexical detectors are exactly those defined in the corresponding appendices: turn-indexed
plans, plan-word and past-word lexicons, coordinate references, imperative verbs, and related markers.
The baseline columns in Table 10 are copied from Tables 9 and 11.

H.2   Quantitative results

Gemini loses much of its planner-like behaviour. Under low reasoning, the memory-entry
features that characterised Gemini in the baseline largely disappear. Entry length falls from 207 to 69
characters, multi-line entries from 71.5% to 2.1%, and turn-indexed plans from 12.4% to 0.1%. The
message channel changes in the same direction: messages fall from 132 to 53 characters, from 3.22
to 1.55 sentences, from 73.7% to 38.7% imperative verb usage, and from 56.5% to 25.6% references
to coordinates. In short, the low-reasoning setting appears to remove much of the outward planning
and directive communication observed in the baseline.

Gemma shifts more deliberation into memory entries. Gemma shows a different pattern. With
reasoning disabled, memory entries become longer rather than shorter (153 → 228 characters),
often approaching the harness’s ∼ 1000-character cap. Their content also becomes more plan-like:
plan-word rate rises from 36.6% to 73.9%, plan terms per entry from 0.52 to 1.28, and past-tense or
reflective tokens from 6.7% to 18.3%. The entries still average roughly one line (1.10 vs. 1.08), so
the change is not toward Gemini-style turn-indexed plans. Instead, Gemma writes longer single-line


                                                       38
deliberations inside the memory field. The messages shift modestly in the same direction, with higher
imperative usage (52.2% → 62.2%) and more plan words (27.0% → 37.6%).


This compensation is incomplete. Although Gemma appears to move some deliberation into the
memory and message fields, performance still falls in the main ablation results: Coord.% drops by
3.3 points and Base% by 2.7 points. Gemini, which does not show the same expansion of memory
entries, loses more Coord.% (−7.8 points) and also loses Base% (−3.5 points). Thus, the behavioural
traces match the quantitative ablation: reducing reasoning changes how models use the remaining
harness channels, but those changes do not recover the lost performance.

I.1   Setup

We analyse the per-step message_sent field, which is the open communication channel available to
agents during an episode. We use the same trajectories as in the scratchpad analysis: 10 Gemini 3.1 Pro
episodes and 20 Gemma 4 episodes, resulting in 34,080 and 24,747 agent-step records respectively.

Lexical detectors. To characterise how agents use the channel, we compute a small set of lexical
features from the raw message text. These are deliberately simple probes rather than discourse parsers.
Their purpose is to capture stable differences in communication style, such as whether a message
gives an instruction, names a teammate, asks a question, or records a plan. Table 11 reports the
resulting rates. For completeness, the detectors are defined as follows:

        • Imperative verb. A sentence-initial base-form verb from a fixed action lexicon (move,
          go, do, place, make, drink, eat, attack, give, request, mine, chop, gather, face, wait, stay,
          build, kill, head, bring, get, take, drop, equip, craft, sleep, rest, hold, return, follow, stop,

          count the same verbs when they immediately follow a teammate addressee elsewhere in the
          message. “Imperatives per message” counts all matches; “contains an imperative” is binary.
        • Broadcast phrasing. A leading first-person plural cohortative: let’s, lets, let us, we should,
          we will, we need to, we must, we’ll.
        • Addresses specific teammate. A token of the form “Agent X,” or “Agent_X,” followed by
          a clause, where X ∈ {0, 1, 2}.
        • Acknowledgement. ok, okay, got it, copy, acknowledged, agreed, sounds good, on it, will
          do, roger.
        • Question. A question mark outside coordinate tokens.
        • Politeness. please, thanks, thank you, ty.
        • Urgency. now, urgent, urgently, immediately, asap, critical, critically, hurry, quickly, fast,
          right now.
        • Plan / future-tense word. will, going to, gonna, plan, planning, next, then, after, once,
          when, plus future-tense modal forms (i’ll, we’ll, i will, we will).
        • Past-tense / memory word. tried, failed, was, were, did, didn’t, couldn’t, earlier, before,
          already, just, noticed, saw.
        • Coordinate. A token matching \(?\s*-?\d+\s*,\s*-?\d+\s*\)?, i.e. a pair of integers
          possibly wrapped in parentheses, such as “25, 23” or “(0, −1)”.
        • Self-reference density. Count of i, my, me, i’m, i’ll, mine tokens, normalised per message.
